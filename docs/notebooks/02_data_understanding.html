Identification of Data Sources

Table Table 2.1 summarizes the data sources used in this project, detailing their type, time span, granularity, system of record (SoR), and content. All data is sourced from SAP BI, which extracts it from the underlying systems of record. The table also lists the specific SAP tables used for each data source.

Articles

The Article Master Data is extracted from MARC which contains Plant specific data, this is enhanced with sales organization specific data from MVKE and global article data from MARA, applied filters are shown in Table Table 2.2.

Customers

Customer Master Data is sourced from KNVV, which contains sales-organization-specific information, and is enriched with global attributes from KNA1. This includes customer classification fields such as type (e.g., internal, external, one-time customer, or employee). Customers flagged as employees are excluded from the analysis.

Sales

Sales data is extracted from LIPS, which contains delivery records. Only completed deliveries which are goods-issued are taken into account.

Table Table 2.3 shows the applied filters to select standard sales orders and free-of-charge orders.

Fixed Planned Promotions are excluded

Articles flagged in the Article Master data as Fixed Planned Promotions are excluded because they aren’t forecasted—instead, they’re delivered by contract and thus always achieve 100 % forecast accuracy.

Forecasts

Forecasts are generated monthly for each article–plant–customer combination, with a planning horizon of up to three years. For this research, the focus is on the year 2024, using a forecast horizon of up to 12 months. Consequently, only forecasts generated from 2023 onward are considered, see Figure 5.1 for visual illustration.

Since forecasts are created both before and after the Demand Review, the dataset is split into two versions: pre-Demand Review and post-Demand Review. The pre-Demand Review forecasts are used for the initial analysis, while the post-Demand Review forecasts serve as the basis for final validation.

Time Series Patterns

Certainly! Here’s a rephrased version with a clearer emphasis on seasonal and trend patterns that exponential smoothing (ETS) and Box-Jenkins (ARMA/ARIMA) methods can exploit:

Exploiting Temporal Structure for Forecasting Time series forecasting is most effective when the data exhibits temporal structure—patterns over time that models can learn and project forward. Two key components of this structure are trend (long-term directional movement) and seasonality (recurring cycles, such as weekly demand spikes or annual holiday effects).

Methods like exponential smoothing (ETS) are particularly suited to capture and extrapolate trend and seasonal components through adaptive smoothing techniques. Similarly, Box-Jenkins models—including ARMA and ARIMA—leverage autocorrelation structures in the data, modeling dependencies between past and future values to make informed predictions.

When such temporal patterns are present, these models can generate meaningful forecasts by systematically exploiting the underlying structure. In contrast, when a series lacks trend, seasonality, or autocorrelation—essentially behaving like random noise—forecasting yields little more than projecting the mean or last known value. Thus, effective forecasting depends on the assumption that past behavior offers useful insight into future outcomes.

Figure Figure 2.3 displays the strength of seasonality (y-axis) and trend (x-axis) for each combination of material and customer hierarchy levels. Notably, all levels exhibit clear seasonality (values > 0.64) and a meaningful trend component (values > 0.5), indicating a strong temporal structure—an encouraging sign for effective forecasting.

Forecastability

ABC/XYZ Classification

The ABC/XYZ classification is a commonly used framework for prioritizing items based on their business impact and forecastability. It supports decision-making on which items warrant detailed forecasting and tighter control.

  • ABC: Does the item have sufficient importance to justify close monitoring or forecasting?

  • XYZ: Is the item predictable enough to produce reliable forecasts?

Syntetos-Boylan (SB) Classification

The Syntetos–Boylan (SB) classification extends the ABC/XYZ framework by integrating demand variability and forecastability. It provides a more nuanced understanding of demand patterns and supports the selection of suitable forecasting methods, see (Syntetos et al., 2005).

For BioBalance, the time series data is predominantly a mix of intermittent and smooth demand, with the specific pattern varying by the level of aggregation.

SB_Type Best_fit_Methods
Smooth SES; Holt’s; Holt-Winters (Triple Exp); ARIMA; Moving Average
Erratic Damped exponential; Holt-Winters (if seasonal); ARIMA with volatility modeling
Intermittent Croston; SBA; TSB; bootstrapped quantile models
Lumpy Croston variants + heuristics; simulation; judgmental overlays

Sufficient Historical Data and Pattern Stability Reliable demand classification and effective forecasting require an adequate historical record—ideally covering multiple seasonal cycles and enough observations to separate meaningful patterns from random noise. For instance, capturing annual seasonality in monthly data typically requires at least 2–3 years of history.

In our analysis, SB-class distributions appear stable over time, aside from fluctuations near the lower boundary—particularly in the first few months, where the classification is less reliable due to insufficient data. As such, early observations (less than 6 months) are omitted from interpretation.

Encouragingly, as time series length increases, the proportion of smooth demand series rises. This shift is favorable for forecast accuracy, as smooth series are more predictable and well-suited to traditional forecasting methods, see figure Figure 2.6
